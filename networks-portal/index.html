Slide 5What is a computer network?

A computer network is a system that connects many independent computers/devices so they can share information (data) and resources.

The integration of computers and other devices can be established using either cable (wired) or wireless media.

A network is just devices that can talk to each other and share things, such as files, a printer or an Internet connection.

Analogy: a group of friends in a group chat. Each friend owns their own phone (independent), yet they all share photos and news. The "connection" can be a physical cable (a string between two tin cans) or invisible (Wi-Fi, like shouting across a room).

Slides 6–11Types of networks: PAN, LAN, CAN, MAN, WAN

The five types

  1. PAN (Personal Area Network): the most basic type. Connects devices within a short range, typically around one person: smartphones, tablets, laptops and wearables communicate and share data.
  2. LAN (Local Area Network): the most frequently used. Connects computers through a common communication path, contained in a limited area, i.e. locally. A LAN offers a network within a building.
  3. CAN (Campus Area Network): bigger than a LAN, smaller than a MAN. Used in schools or colleges; covers a limited geographical area, spreading across several buildings within the campus.
  4. MAN (Metropolitan Area Network): larger than a LAN, smaller than a WAN. Connects computers over a geographical distance through a shared communication path over a city, town or metropolitan area.
  5. WAN (Wide Area Network): connects computers over a large geographical distance through a shared communication path; not restrained to a single location, extends over many locations. It can also be defined as a group of LANs communicating with each other over a range above 50 km.

The only difference is how big an area the network covers. Remember the order: Personal < Local < Campus < Metropolitan < Wide.

  • PAN = your backpack. Your phone, earbuds, watch and laptop around you.
  • LAN = your house. Everything inside one building shares one network.
  • CAN = a university campus. Several buildings, one owner.
  • MAN = a whole city. Offices, homes and government buildings linked across town.
  • WAN = a country or the world. Many LANs joined over long distances (more than 50 km). Like many neighbourhoods linked by long highways.

Slides 12–14Introduction to the Internet: "nuts and bolts"

  • The Internet is a network of networks: a computer network interconnecting billions of computing devices throughout the world.
  • The Internet is a Wide Area Network (WAN).

"Nuts and bolts" view (slide 13)

  • Billions of connected computing devices: hosts = end systems, running network applications at the Internet's "edge".
  • Packet switches forward packets (chunks of data): routers and switches.
  • Communication links: fiber, copper, radio, satellite; transmission rate = bandwidth.
  • Networks: a collection of devices, routers and links managed by an organization.

The slide's picture contains: a mobile network, a home network, an enterprise network, a local or regional ISP, a national or global ISP, a content-provider network and a datacenter network, all joined by routers.

The Internet is not one giant computer. It is thousands of separate networks (each run by someone different) that agreed to connect.

Analogy: the world's road system. Each country, city and company builds its own roads, but they join at borders and junctions so you can drive from anywhere to anywhere.

  • Hosts / end systems = the houses at the end of the road (phones, laptops, servers).
  • Routers/switches = junctions and roundabouts that point each package the right way.
  • Packets = parcels: your data is cut into small pieces.
  • Links = the roads themselves: fiber, copper wire, radio waves, satellite. Bandwidth = how many lanes the road has (how fast it can carry data).
  • A network = one organization's patch of road and junctions.

  • Network edge: hosts are clients and servers; servers are often in data centers.
  • Access networks, physical media: wired and wireless communication links that connect edge hosts to the first router.
  • Network core: interconnected routers; the network of networks.

The slide's picture circles, in turn: the hosts (phones, laptops, servers); then the links and the first router near them (home router, base station, enterprise switches); then the routers of the local/regional and national/global ISPs.

Split the Internet into three zones, like a city:

  • Edge = the houses. Where people and servers live (phones, laptops, big server computers in warehouses called data centers). Clients ask for things, servers supply them.
  • Access network = your street. The short cable or wireless link that joins each house to the city's main road.
  • Core = the highway system. A mesh of big junctions (routers) that carry traffic between cities.

Plus the "rules and tools": protocols (traffic rules), applications (the reason anyone travels: web, email, video) and hardware and software (the vehicles and the drivers).

Slides 19–20What is a protocol?

A protocol defines the format and the order of messages exchanged between two or more communicating entities, as well as the actions taken on the transmission and/or receipt of a message or other event.

All activities in the Internet that involve two or more communicating remote entities are governed by a protocol. TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) and IP (Internet Protocol) are two of the most important protocols in the Internet.

A protocol is the rules of a conversation: who speaks first, how a message must look, and what to do when you hear it.

Analogy: a phone call. You say "Hi", the other person says "Hi" (now you are both connected), you ask "Got the time?", they answer "2:00". If you skipped "Hi" or spoke a language they don't know, the conversation would fail.

Computers do exactly the same: the browser first asks to open a connection, the server agrees, the browser asks for a page ("GET"), and the server sends back the file. TCP and IP are the two most important rule-books of the Internet.

  • Uses the existing telephone line to the central office DSLAM (DSL Access Multiplexer).
  • Data over the DSL phone line goes to the Internet; voice over the DSL phone line goes to the telephone network.
  • Voice and data are transmitted at different frequencies over a dedicated line to the central office.

Slides 3–5Links and physical media

Basic definitions

  • bit: propagates between transmitter/receiver pairs.
  • physical link: what lies between transmitter and receiver.
  • guided media: signals propagate in solid media: copper, fiber, coax.
  • unguided media: signals propagate freely, e.g. radio.

A bit (a 0 or a 1) must travel from sender to receiver. The "physical link" is whatever is in between.

  • Guided = the signal is kept inside a cable, like water inside a pipe.
  • Unguided = the signal spreads through the air, like sound from a loudspeaker.

Slides 6–10The network core: routing and forwarding

  • The network core is a mesh of interconnected routers.
  • Packet switching: hosts break application-layer messages into packets; the network forwards packets from one router to the next, across links on the path from source to destination.
  • The network core has two key functions: routing and forwarding.

Routing

A global action: determine the source-destination path taken by packets, using routing algorithms.

Forwarding (aka "switching")

A local action: move arriving packets from the router's input link to the appropriate router output link, using a local forwarding table (header value → output link).

Slides 11–13Data transmission methods: circuit switching

Two methods transfer data between devices in a network: 1) circuit switching, 2) packet switching.

Circuit switching

  • A dedicated communication path (circuit) is established between two devices before data transmission begins.
  • The circuit stays dedicated for the duration of the session; no other device can use it meanwhile. Commonly used in voice communication.
  • End-to-end resources allocated; dedicated resources: no sharing.
  • A circuit segment is idle if not used by the call (no sharing).
  • Commonly used in traditional telephone networks and radio channels.

Circuit switching = booking a private road before you travel.

Analogy: an old telephone call. Before you talk, the phone company reserves a line for you from end to end. While you talk (even in silence) nobody else may use that line. Pro: reliable and steady. Con: if you stay quiet, the reserved road sits empty and wasted.

Another picture: reserving a restaurant table for the whole evening even if you only eat for ten minutes.

FDM vs TDM (4 users)

Two ways to share a link's capacity among circuits:

  • Frequency Division Multiplexing (FDM): each user gets its own frequency band continuously, for the whole time.
  • Time Division Multiplexing (TDM): time is split into repeating slots; each user gets the whole frequency band during its own slot (users 1,2,3,4,1,2,3,4,…).
FDM (frequency vs time)
TDM (time slots)

Four friends share one road.

  • FDM = four lanes. Each friend permanently owns one lane (a slice of the road's width) and drives in it all the time.
  • TDM = one wide lane with turns. The whole road is used by one friend at a time, in a fixed rotation: you, you, then it is friend 2's turn, then friend 3, then friend 4, and back to you.

The slide's picture uses four colours: each colour is one user. In FDM the colours are stacked in bands; in TDM they alternate along the time axis. Either way, each user's share is reserved, even when it is idle.

Slides 14, 19, 20Packet switching and how a host sends packets

  • Data is divided into smaller units called packets, transmitted over the network. Each packet contains the source and destination addresses, plus other information needed for routing.
  • Hosts break application-layer data into packets; packets are forwarded from one router to the next, across the links on the path from source to destination.
  • Efficient use of bandwidth: bandwidth is shared among multiple users.
  • Resources are allocated only when data needs to be transmitted.

Host sending function (slide 19)

  • Takes the application message → breaks it into smaller chunks, packets of length L bits.
  • Transmits the packet into the access network at transmission rate R (also called link capacity or link bandwidth).
packet transmission delay = L (bits) ÷ R (bits/sec)

It is the time needed to push all L bits of the packet into the link, per hop.

Packet switching = posting letters instead of booking a private road.

Analogy: you cut a long message into small parcels. Each parcel has a label (From / To). All parcels use the shared public roads; each junction reads the label and sends the parcel onward. Roads are only used when there are parcels to carry, so nobody's road sits empty.

  • The host does the cutting: message → packets of L bits each.
  • It sends them out at speed R (bits per second), like the speed of a conveyor belt out of your house.
  • Time to put one whole parcel on the belt = size ÷ speed = L ÷ R.

Slides 16, 20, 21, 22Store-and-forward and transmission delay

  • Store-and-forward: the entire packet must arrive at the router before it can be transmitted on the next link.
  • Packet transmission delay: L/R seconds to push an L-bit packet into a link of rate R bps.
  • Transmission time is computed per hop.

Slide example 1 (one hop)

L = 10 Kbits = 10 × 10³ = 10,000 bits
R = 100 Mbps = 100 × 10⁶ = 100,000,000 bits/s
L / R = 10,000 / 100,000,000 = 10⁴ / 10⁸ = 10⁻⁴ s
= 0.1 ms (one-hop transmission delay)

Slide example 2 (one hop and two hops)

L = 7.5 Mbits = 7,500,000 bits; R = 1.5 Mbps = 1,500,000 bits/s
One hop: L / R = 7.5 / 1.5 = 5 s
Two hops (source → router → destination): 2 × 5 = 10 s

Two hops cost twice as much because the router must receive the whole packet (5 s) before it can start sending it on (another 5 s).

Store-and-forward = the post-office rule: the clerk waits until the whole parcel has arrived before sending it on. He cannot forward half a parcel.

Time to send = size ÷ speed.

Example 1: a 10,000-bit packet on a link that moves 100,000,000 bits each second.
10,000 ÷ 100,000,000 = 0.0001 s = 0.1 millisecond. Super fast.

  • Queueing occurs when work arrives faster than it can be serviced (the slide's pictures: a toll plaza, a line of people, a crowded bus stop).

Mismatch in the picture: 100 Mb/s in, 1.5 Mb/s out → 100 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 66.7× faster in than out, so the queue grows quickly while A sends flat out.

Analogy: a wide four-lane highway (100 Mb/s) that suddenly narrows into a tiny one-lane bridge (1.5 Mb/s). Cars pile up before the bridge: that waiting line is the queue.

The waiting area is the router's buffer (its memory), and it has limited size. When the waiting area is full, new arrivals are turned away (dropped = packet loss).

100 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 66.7: the highway delivers about 67 cars for every 1 car the bridge can pass.

Slides 2–5Network performance parameters

Agenda: network performance parameters (delay, loss, throughput); network security; protocol layers and service models (Internet protocol stack and ISO/OSI model).

Good performance is measured by three parameters: 1) loss, 2) delay, 3) throughput.

  • A queue in computer networks is a collection of data packets collectively waiting to be transmitted by a network device, using a pre-defined structure methodology.
  • In the slide's router picture: the packet being transmitted → delay; packets queueing → delay; free (available) buffers: arriving packets are dropped (loss) if there are no free buffers.
  • Network congestion (overloading) leads to network delay and packet loss.
  • Network delay occurs if the queue is large and each packet must wait for delivery under excessive traffic.
  • Packet loss occurs when the network discards packets because the queue reached its maximum capacity and cannot accommodate more packets.

Three questions tell you if a network is "good":

  • Loss: do my packets get lost on the way?
  • Delay: how long does a packet take?
  • Throughput: how fast can data actually flow (bits per second)?

Analogy: a post office counter with a waiting room. Parcels wait in the waiting room (the queue). If too many parcels come in at once (congestion), they wait a long time (delay), and when the waiting room is completely full the extra parcels are thrown out (loss).

Slide 6Loss

  • The queue preceding a link (in the buffer) has finite capacity.
  • A packet arriving to a full queue is dropped (lost).
  • A lost packet may be retransmitted by the previous node (large network) or by the source end system (small network), depending on the type of network.

Slide picture: hosts A and B → router with a full buffer (waiting area) → the packet being transmitted; the packet arriving to the full buffer is lost.

The waiting room has a fixed number of seats. If it is full and another parcel arrives, that parcel is dropped.

The dropped parcel can be re-sent:

  • In a big network, the previous post office resends it.
  • In a small network, the original sender resends it.

Slides 7–9Delay: the four components

There are four types of delay at each node (hop):

  1. Processing (dproc): time for the router to read the packet header (source, destination, length L).
  2. Queueing (dqueue): waiting time of the packet in the queue.
  3. Transmission (dtrans): time to get all the bits into the wire: packet length L ÷ transmission rate R. L = packet length (bits); R = link bandwidth (bps).
  4. Propagation (dprop): time for one bit to travel from one end of the wire to the other; proportional to the wire's length. d = length of the physical link (metres); s = propagation speed (to be given, or assume ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s).
dnodal = dproc + dqueue + dtrans + dprop
dtrans = L / R     dprop = d / s
  • Queueing and transmission are the most dominant delays; processing and propagation are very small values (per the slide).
  • Transmission time and propagation time are NOT the same. Transmission depends on packet length L; propagation depends on wire length.
Slide note"Very small" is a typical case only. In Exercise 1 below, propagation (4 ms) is half the transmission time (8 ms), and for satellites it is huge. Always compute both.

Queueing time depends on R (link bandwidth, bps), L (packet length, bits), N (number of packets). Queueing time of the N-th packet:

Dqueue(N) = (N − 1) × (L / R)

(All packets assumed equal length, arriving together at an idle link; the N-th packet waits for the N − 1 packets ahead of it.)

Unit hints: 1 kbps = 10³ bits/s; 1 Mbps = 10⁶ bits/s; 1 Gbps = 10⁹ bits/s. 1 byte = 8 bits.

A packet loses time at each router for four different reasons. Think of mailing a parcel through one post office:

  1. Processing = the clerk reads the label (very quick).
  2. Queueing = waiting in line behind other parcels.
  3. Transmission = the time to push all of the parcel's bits out of the door: size ÷ speed of the door. A bigger parcel or a narrower door = longer.
  4. Propagation = the time to travel down the road to the next office: road length ÷ speed of travel.

Total delay at one stop = all four added up.

Do not mix up transmission and propagation! Transmission is about how big the parcel is. Propagation is about how far it has to go. A tiny parcel on a long road has small transmission but large propagation.

Waiting in line: if you are person number N, there are N − 1 people ahead of you. Each takes L ÷ R to serve. So you wait (N − 1) × (L ÷ R).

Unit cheat-sheet: kilo = 1,000; mega = 1,000,000; giga = 1,000,000,000. One byte = 8 bits, so multiply bytes by 8 to get bits.

Traffic intensity = how busy the road is compared with how many cars it can pass.

It is "bits arriving per second" ÷ "bits the link can send per second":

  • Close to 0 = an empty highway: you drive straight through (tiny wait).

The curve is flat, then shoots upward near 1: a road can feel fine at 50% full and suddenly awful at 95%.

Same numbers: 100 packets a second of 1,000 bits = 100,000 bits/s, on a road that carries 1,000,000 bits/s: that road is 10% used → 0.1.

Slides 17–18"Real" Internet delays and routes: traceroute

The traceroute program measures delay from the source to each router along the end-to-end Internet path towards the destination. For every router i:

  • It sends three packets that will reach router i on the path (with a time-to-live (TTL) field value of i).
  • Router i returns the packets to the sender.
  • The sender measures the time interval between transmission and reply.

Slide example: gaia.cs.umass.edu → www.eurecom.fr. Each line shows 3 delay measurements (e.g. hop 1: from gaia.cs.umass.edu to cs-gw.cs.umass.edu). "*" means no response (probe lost, router not replying). Hop 7→8 is the trans-oceanic link (22 ms → ~106 ms). The slide asks: "looks like delays decrease! Why?" (hops 11 and 12 are highlighted).

Textbook answerEach of the three probes is a separate packet sent at a different moment, so each sees different queueing. The delay to router i measures only that router, and a later router can reply faster than an earlier one if the earlier one's probes were queued more.

traceroute is a tool that lists every stop along the way to a website and how long each took, like asking each bus stop on the route to wave back and timing the reply.

How it works: it sends 3 little test packets for each stop number i, with a "stop here" counter (time-to-live) set to i. The i-th router sees that the counter ran out, sends a reply, and your computer times the round trip.

  • The first stops (inside the university) take 1–2 ms.
  • Crossing the ocean costs a big jump: about 22 ms → about 106 ms.
  • "* * *" means "no answer" (the test packet got lost, or that router stays silent).
  • Sometimes a later stop looks faster than an earlier one. That is because each test is a separate trip made at a different moment, and queues change second by second.

Three questions today: how do attackers get in, how do we defend, and how do we build systems that cannot be attacked.

  • Virus = a cold you catch from someone. It spreads only when a person does something (opens an attachment) and it hides inside another program.
  • Worm = an airborne bug. It spreads by itself with no one clicking, as a stand-alone program, so it spreads faster.

Attack 1: packet "sniffing" (packet interception, slide 25)

On broadcast media (shared Ethernet, wireless), a promiscuous network interface reads/records all packets passing by, including passwords. Slide picture: B sends a packet (src:B, dest:A, payload); attacker C on the same medium copies it. Wireshark, used in the course's end-of-chapter labs, is a free packet sniffer.

Attack 2: IP spoofing (fake identity, slide 26)

Injection of a packet with a false source address: C sends a packet to A claiming src: B.

Attack 3: Denial of Service (DoS, slide 27)

Attackers make resources (server, bandwidth) unavailable to legitimate traffic by overwhelming the resource with bogus traffic. Steps: 1) select target; 2) break into hosts around the network (see botnet); 3) send packets to the target from the compromised hosts.

  • Sniffing = eavesdropping in a noisy café. In wireless or shared networks everyone's messages "float by" every device. A sneaky listener (a promiscuous interface) writes down everything, even passwords.
  • IP spoofing = a fake return address on an envelope. The attacker (C) pretends to be B when writing to A.
  • Denial of service = a thousand people phoning one shop at once. Real customers cannot get through. The attacker first takes over many innocent computers (a botnet), then makes them all flood the target together.

Lines of defense (slide 28)

  • Authentication: proving you are who you say you are. Cellular networks provide hardware identity via the SIM card; there is no such hardware assist in the traditional Internet.
  • Confidentiality: via encryption.
  • Integrity checks: digital signatures prevent/detect tampering.
  • Access restrictions: password-protected VPNs.
  • Firewalls: specialised "middleboxes" in access and core networks. Off-by-default: filter incoming packets to restrict senders, receivers and applications; detect/react to DoS attacks.

More in Chapter 8. (Slide 24 repeats slide 22's "Internet not originally designed with security" text.)

  • Authentication = showing your ID. Your phone's SIM card is a built-in ID badge; ordinary Internet computers do not have one.
  • Confidentiality (encryption) = writing in a secret code. Even if someone reads it, it looks like nonsense.
  • Integrity (digital signature) = a wax seal. If the seal is broken, you know the letter was changed.
  • Access restrictions (VPN) = a members-only door with a password.
  • Firewall = a security guard at the gate. Everything is blocked by default; only allowed senders, receivers and programs are let in, and the guard shouts if a flood arrives.

Slides 29–34Protocol layers and reference models

Networks are complex, with many "pieces": hosts, routers, links of various media, applications, protocols, hardware, software. Question: is there any hope of organizing the structure of a network (and/or our discussion of networks)? Answer: layering.

Air-travel analogy (slides 31–32)

End-to-end transfer of person plus baggage. At the departure side: ticket (purchase), baggage (check), gates (load), runway takeoff, airplane routing; in the middle: airplane routing; at the arrival side: ticket (complain), baggage (claim), gates (unload), runway landing, airplane routing. Each layer implements a service via its own internal-layer actions, relying on services provided by the layer below.

Why layering? (slide 33)

  • Explicit structure allows identification and relationship of the system's pieces (a layered reference model for discussion).
  • Modularization eases maintenance and updating: a change in a layer's service implementation is transparent to the rest of the system (e.g. a change in gate procedure doesn't affect the rest of the system).

A network has so many parts that we need a way to organise it. The trick is layers: split the big job into small steps, each step doing one thing and using the step underneath.

Analogy: a flight. Buy a ticket → check bags → board → take off → fly. Each step is a separate service; each relies on the one below it (you can't take off without a boarded plane). At the destination you do the steps backwards (land, unload, collect bags, complain about the ticket!).

Why bother?

  • It gives everyone the same map ("we're talking about the transport layer").
  • You can change one step without breaking the others: if the airport changes how boarding works, the ticket office does not care.

Slides 35–40, 43Services, layering and encapsulation

  • The application layer exchanges messages (M) using the services of the transport layer.
  • The transport layer protocol encapsulates the application message M with a transport header Ht to create a segment [Ht | M]; it transfers M (e.g. reliably) from one process to another, using the services of the network layer. Ht is used by the transport protocol to implement its service.
  • The network layer encapsulates the segment with header Hn to create a datagram [Hn | Ht | M]; it transfers it from one host to another, using link-layer services.
  • The link layer encapsulates the datagram with header Hl to create a frame [Hl | Hn | Ht | M]; it transfers it from host to a neighbouring host.
  • At the destination the headers are removed layer by layer, upward, until M is delivered to the application.
  • End-to-end view (slide 40): the source uses all 5 layers; a switch has only link + physical (it reads Hl); a router has network + link + physical (it reads Hn); the destination uses all 5 layers.
Slide wordingSlide 37 says the link-layer protocol transfers the datagram "using network-layer services". Following the rule "each layer relies on the layer below", it uses physical-layer services.

Encapsulation = packing a gift in boxes. Each layer adds its own labelled box around what it received:

  • Application writes the message (the gift).
  • Transport wraps it in a box with a label (Ht) → segment.
  • Network wraps that in a bigger box with a delivery label (Hn) → datagram.
  • Link wraps again (Hl) → frame.

Analogy: Russian matryoshka dolls: message (smallest doll) → segment → datagram → frame (largest). At the other end, each layer opens one doll and passes the rest up, until the application gets the original gift.

On the way: a switch only looks at the outermost label (link), a router also reads the next label in (network) to decide the direction. Only the two end computers open all the boxes.

Application layer: models, processes, sockets, requirements, TCP/UDP

The first lecture of Chapter 2: how network applications are structured, how processes find and talk to each other, what applications need from the network, and which transport protocol they use.

Slides 2–8Introduction to the application layer

Agenda: introduction; application models (client-server and peer-to-peer); process communication; addressing processes; messaging properties; application requirements; transport layer protocols; application layer security. ("Messaging properties" is what an application-layer protocol defines, below.)

  • We start the application layer (Chapter 2) of the 5-layer stack.
  • Network applications are programs that run on (different) end systems and communicate over the network, e.g. web-server software communicating with browser software.
  • There is no need to write software for network-core devices: routers and switches do not run user applications. Having applications only on end systems allows rapid app development and propagation.

Examples of application-layer programs: e-mail, web, text messaging, file sharing, multi-user network games, streaming stored video (YouTube, Netflix).

Application-layer protocol examples: HTTP (Hypertext Transfer Protocol), FTP (File Transfer Protocol), SMTP (Simple Mail Transfer Protocol).

An application-layer protocol defines

  • Types of messages exchanged, e.g. request, response.
  • Message syntax: what fields are in messages and how fields are delineated.
  • Message semantics: the meaning of the information in the fields.
  • Rules for when and how processes send and respond to messages.

This lecture is about the apps: the things you actually use (WhatsApp, Netflix, a web browser).

To make a new app you only write programs for the end computers (your phone and the company's server). You never need to program the routers in the middle: they just pass packets along, like a postman who delivers letters without reading or acting on them. That is why new apps can appear and spread so quickly.

Examples: email, web, texting, file sharing, online multiplayer games, YouTube/Netflix.

Each app speaks a "language" called an application-layer protocol: HTTP for web pages, FTP for moving files, SMTP for sending email.

A language needs four things:

  • Kinds of sentences: questions (requests) and answers (responses).
  • Grammar: which fields a message has and how to tell where one field ends.
  • Meaning: what each field means.
  • Etiquette: who speaks when and how to reply.

Slides 9–12Application models: client-server and peer-to-peer

There are two structures (models) of applications:

  1. Client-Server: the broadly used network model, in which clients and server are differentiated.
  2. Peer-to-Peer (P2P): does not differentiate clients and servers; each node is itself client and server.

Client-server characteristics

Server: always online; permanent IP address; supports scaling; data management is centralised.

Client: communicates with the server; may be intermittently connected; has dynamic IP addresses; does not communicate directly with other clients.

P2P characteristics

  • End systems/hosts act as both servers and clients (example: file-sharing programs) and communicate directly.
  • Peers request service from other peers and provide service in return.
  • Self-scalability: new peers bring new service capacity as well as new service demands.
  • Peers are intermittently connected and change IP addresses.
  • Complex management.
  • Client-server = a restaurant. The kitchen (server) is always open, has a fixed address, and keeps everything in one place. Customers (clients) come and go, have no fixed seat, and never serve each other. More customers? Build a bigger kitchen (scale the server).
  • Peer-to-peer = a potluck dinner. Everyone brings food and everyone eats. Each person is both customer and cook, so more guests mean more food (it "scales itself"). But nobody is in charge, people drop in and out, and keeping track of who has what is complicated.

Examples: web browsing = client-server; file sharing programs = P2P.

Slides 13–14Process communication and sockets

  • Process: a program running within a host.
  • Processes in different hosts communicate by exchanging messages (client-server model).
  • In the client-server model: the client process is the process that initiates communication; the server process is the one that waits to be contacted.
  • In the P2P model, applications have both client processes and server processes.

Sockets

  • A process sends/receives messages to/from its socket. A socket is analogous to a door: the sending process shoves the message out the door and relies on the transport infrastructure on the other side of the door to deliver the message to the socket at the receiving process. Two sockets are involved: one on each side.
  • Slide picture: the process (application layer) is controlled by the app developer; the socket sits at the boundary, and the transport, network, link and physical layers below it are controlled by the OS.
  • Process = a program that is running right now (your browser is a process; the web-server program is another).
  • Programs on different computers talk by sending messages.
  • The one who starts the chat is the client process; the one who sits waiting for someone to call is the server process (like a shop waiting for customers).
  • In P2P each program does both.

Socket = a door in your program's wall. You push the message out of your door and trust the delivery system (the transport layer, run by the operating system) to bring it to the matching door on the other computer. Two doors: one at each end.

The app developer builds the room and the door (the app side); the operating system builds and runs everything behind the door (the delivery service).

Slides 19–20Transport layer protocols: TCP and UDP

1. Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) supports:

  • Reliability: reliable transport between sending and receiving process.
  • Flow control: the sender won't overwhelm the receiver.
  • Congestion control: throttle the sender when the network is overloaded.
  • TCP does not support timing or throughput guarantees.

2. User Datagram Protocol (UDP) supports:

  • "Slightly support" timing and throughput (slide wording).
  • Unreliable data transfer between sending and receiving process.
  • Does not support: reliability, flow control, congestion control, security, or connection setup.
Slide notePer the textbook, neither TCP nor UDP guarantees timing or throughput. UDP's advantage is that it adds no setup, no retransmission waits and no congestion throttling, so it can be faster and smoother for real-time apps.

Two delivery services; the app picks one:

  • TCP = registered courier. It guarantees the package arrives (reliability), doesn't deliver faster than the receiver can handle (flow control), and slows down when the roads are jammed (congestion control). It does not promise how fast.
  • UDP = dropping a postcard in a mailbox. Fast and simple, no promises: it might get lost, there's no tracking, no calling ahead (no connection setup), no slowing down for traffic, no secrecy.

Slide 21Application layer security

  • Both TCP and UDP do not support data encryption.
  • Question: how to secure the transferred data packets?
  • Answer: the Secure Socket Layer (SSL) protocol, implemented at the application layer.
  • SSL provides an encrypted TCP connection, data integrity and end-point authentication.
  • A website that implements SSL has "HTTPS" in its URL instead of "HTTP".
Textbook noteSSL's modern successor is called TLS; the idea is the same.

Neither courier (TCP) nor postcard (UDP) hides your message. Anyone who handles it could read it.

The fix is a sealed, coded envelope added by the app itself: SSL. It gives you three things:

  • Secret (encrypted connection),
  • Untampered (integrity: the contents were not changed),
  • Right person (authentication: you are really talking to the real website).

Slides 4–6The HTTP protocol, TCP and round-trip time

  • Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP) is an application-layer protocol. HTTP messages in the client/server model are either a request or a response.
  • Client request: the browser requests, receives responses from web servers (using HTTP) and "displays" web objects.
  • Server response: the web server sends objects (using HTTP) in response to clients' requests. Slide picture: a PC running Firefox and an iPhone running Safari send requests to a server running the Apache web server.

Using HTTP with TCP

The TCP connection is established in 3 steps (slide wording):

  1. The client initiates a TCP connection (creates a socket) to the server at port 80.
  2. The server accepts the TCP connection from the client.
  3. HTTP messages are exchanged between the browser (HTTP client) and the web server (HTTP server).

HTTP is "stateless": the server has no information about past client requests.

Textbook noteThe three-way handshake proper is SYN → SYN-ACK → ACK (the client's ACK is combined with the first HTTP request). The slide's 3 steps are the HTTP-over-TCP sequence. The "1 RTT to set up the TCP connection" used in the calculations below is that handshake.

Round-trip time (RTT)

  • RTT: time for a small packet to travel from client to server and back.
  • RTT = 2 × propagation time (a tiny packet has negligible transmission time).
  • Total transmission time = initial request transmission time + web-page objects transmission time.
  • Initial request transmission time = 2 RTT: one for the TCP connection request and one for the HTML base file.

HTTP = the ordering language of the web. The browser (client) asks, the web server answers. Two message kinds only: request and response.

Opening a conversation (with TCP): 1) knock on the server's door (port 80); 2) the server opens; 3) now you may talk (request/response).

Stateless = a waiter with no memory. Every time you speak, the server treats you like a stranger: it does not remember what you asked a second ago. (Cookies fix this, below.)

RTT = a ping-pong ball. The time for a tiny message to go to the server and come back. It is simply twice the one-way travel time.

One-way trip = 40 ms → RTT = 2 × 40 = 80 ms.

Getting a page costs:

  • 1 RTT to open the connection (knock, door opens),
  • 1 RTT to ask for the base file and get the first reply,
  • so the very first stage costs 2 RTT, plus each object's own transfer time.

Slides 7–10Non-persistent HTTP connections

Two kinds of HTTP connections:

  1. Non-persistent: for each object a new connection is created to send that object from source to destination.
  2. Persistent: all objects are sent using one opened connection from source to destination.

Non-persistent HTTP response time (per object)

  • 1 RTT to initiate the TCP connection;
  • 1 RTT for the HTTP request and the first few bytes of the HTTP response to return;
  • plus the object/file transmission time.
non-persistent HTTP response time = 2 RTT + file transmission time

Slides 11–14Persistent HTTP connections and the comparison

Disadvantage: network resources may be kept occupied even when not needed and may not be available to others.

Persistent = one long phone call. Dial once, then keep the line open and ask many questions.

Good: less computer effort, fewer connections clogging the network, no re-dialling delay for later requests.

Bad: an open but silent line still ties up resources that others could use.

  • A blank line = "end of my details". \r\n is just "end of line" (carriage return + new line).

The general format is: method, address, version → many name: value lines → blank line → optional body.

The four verbs:

  • GET = "show me this page".
  • POST = "here is a filled-in form (in the body), please process it".
  • HEAD = "just tell me about the page (headers), don't send it" (the slide says "update the web page"; see the red note).
  • DELETE = "remove this file".

Slides 24–28User-server state: cookies

  • A cookie is a small piece of data that a server sends to a user's web browser. The browser may store it and send it back to the same server with later requests.
  • Cookies are the way to solve HTTP's stateless feature.

Steps of cookies

  1. The client requests the HTTP web page for the first time.
  2. The web server creates a cookie and puts it in the header line of the response (set-cookie:).
  3. The cookie ID is saved in the back-end database at the server.
  4. The cookie file is kept on the user's host and managed by the user's browser.
  5. The browser attaches a cookie header line (cookie:) to following HTTP requests.

Example: you always access the Internet from your PC; on visiting a specific e-commerce site for the first time, when the initial HTTP request arrives the site creates a unique ID and an entry in the back-end database for that ID. The cookie is sent in the response message and will be sent with further requests.

Slide diagram: the client's cookie file already has "ebay 8734". The usual HTTP request goes to the Amazon server, which creates ID 1678 and a DB entry. The response carries set-cookie: 1678; the cookie file now has "ebay 8734, amazon 1678". The next request carries cookie: 1678 and the server performs a cookie-specific action. One week later, the same cookie again triggers the cookie-specific action.

Cookies can be used for: authorization, shopping carts, recommendations.

HTTP forgets you after every request. A cookie is a name tag or loyalty card the website hands you at your first visit.

  1. First visit: the shop doesn't know you.
  2. The shop makes you a card with a number (e.g. 1678) and writes the same number in its own customer notebook (the back-end database).
  3. Your browser keeps the card in its wallet (the cookie file); you can have cards from many shops (eBay 8734, Amazon 1678).
  4. Every time you return, the browser shows the card: "Hi, I'm 1678." The shop looks you up and continues where you stopped, even a week later.

What it is used for: staying logged in (authorization), remembering what is in your shopping cart, and suggesting products (recommendations).

Slides 29–31Web caching: proxy server

  • A proxy server is a server application that provides a gateway between users and the Internet.
  • Goal: satisfy the client request without involving the origin server.
  • The user sets the browser so that Web access goes via the cache; the browser sends all HTTP requests to the cache:
    • object in cache: the cache returns the object;
    • else: the cache requests the object from the origin server, then returns it to the client.
  • The cache acts as both client and server: server for the original requesting client, client to the origin server.
  • A proxy is installed by an ISP (university, company, residential ISP).

Why web caching?

  • Reduces response time for client requests (the cache is closer to the client).
  • Reduces traffic on an institution's access link.
  • Prevents cyber attackers from entering a private network (slide's point).
  • The Internet is dense with caches, enabling "poor" content providers to deliver content more effectively.

The server tells the cache about an object's allowable caching in a response header: Cache-Control: max-age=<seconds> or Cache-Control: no-cache.

A proxy server (web cache) = a corner shop that stocks popular items so you don't travel to the factory (the origin server) every time.

  • You always ask the corner shop first.
  • It has it? It hands it over right away (fast, and the factory is never bothered).
  • It doesn't? The shop acts as a customer, orders from the factory, keeps a copy on its shelf, then gives you yours. Next person who asks gets it from the shelf.

The shop is a server when talking to you and a client when talking to the factory.

Why: faster answers (the shop is nearby), less traffic on the school's/company's expensive connection to the outside world, and it also acts as a gatekeeper between a private network and the Internet.

The factory can add labels to a product: "max-age = N seconds" (keep it for N seconds) or "no-cache" (don't reuse it without checking).
